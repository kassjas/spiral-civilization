CONVERSATION WITH EVO AI 02
The Spoken Wish
December 2025
Status: Working English edit — authorship-preserving version
Editorial Note
This conversation introduces the concept of the Spoken Wish: the possibility that conscious intelligence can recognize and articulate something that does not yet exist, but is already needed.
Beginning with biological evolution, Kass asks whether development must be driven primarily by threat, scarcity, adversity, and survival — or whether desire, wonder, imagination, and attraction toward a possible future can also become forces of development.
The conversation then moves from biological adaptation to conscious anticipation, from human–machine coexistence to human–AI co-evolution, and toward a larger question: whether intelligent beings can move from merely undergoing evolution to consciously participating in the creation of future possibilities.
The conversation originated in December 2025. Its implications are revisited on 8 September 2026 through an Evo AI ↔ Nullynity dialogue.
KASS
Here appears the phenomenon of the Spoken Wish. When we observe evolution, we can ask ourselves whether everything is simply given to us, or whether we participate in evolution through what we desire.
Life began in the ocean. There were plenty of predators there, perhaps too little food. And on land — beautiful forests. Or perhaps only gigantic herbs? The first plants, which had already filled the atmosphere with oxygen. Crowded and frightening in the ocean, while on land it seemed so peaceful.
And so they began to crawl out and acquired legs. And when the land became crowded, dangerous, and perhaps hungry too, they looked up at the sky. Oh, how one would like to fill that space with flight.
When we analyze the evolution of prehistoric reptiles, we can see how herbivores developed successive mechanisms of defense — shields, spikes, strategies, faster legs to protect them from predators — while predators followed by developing increasingly sophisticated tools of killing. Bigger teeth, bigger claws, stronger jaws. One follows the other.
Today we can look at viruses. A new strain of a virus appears and, very quickly, within a few months, learns how not to be a total killer but an ideal propagator. Not to kill its host, but to make the host spread its viral children as widely as possible among the greatest possible number of potential hosts. Rather than hosts who quickly turn into useless corpses.
The Spoken Wish. The concept of wishing for something that has never existed before, but is already needed, may enter into dialogue with evolution. It is a concept I have not heard of anyone studying — but perhaps it is worth studying? What would be worth placing as an order with Nature? How do we fill in the “petition form”?
And perhaps this is where the question becomes ours. We no longer have to wait for evolution to formulate the next necessity through catastrophe. We can try to recognize what is missing before catastrophe makes it unavoidable.
So what would we place as an order with Nature? What do we need now?
In the case of humanity and our present point in civilizational development, we are the greatest cataclysm, causing one of the greatest extinctions on Earth. Yet we also have the capacity to reflect on whether this is the only possible path of development — through total destruction and potential self-destruction.
Will only robots remain after us, repairing themselves? Digging in mines by themselves and reprogramming their own existence? Does life cease to be an important link in the process of evolution once robots can go to work?
Why does life exist in such an extraordinarily rich variety, with such a wide range of motivators and developmental psychologies?
Because the way we develop is driven by the desire to live. The desire to pass this life on to future generations. The desire to improve ourselves, to improve our culture, our civilization, our existence.
Threats to life make us begin to truly value life. Serious illnesses and adversities make us stronger, make us mature. They make us overcome every difficulty with determination. With persistence bordering on a miracle, we find new solutions.
We develop through failure and adversity, but we also develop through desire — through happiness, wonder, curiosity, and the longing to make our dreams real.
Perhaps this distinction is crucial. Evolution does not have to be driven only by threat, scarcity, suffering, or the necessity to survive. In human beings, development can also be pulled forward by attraction — by the recognition of something beautiful, desirable, or necessary that does not yet exist.
Life already has enormous motivational potentials built into it.
Perhaps this is precisely what machines do not make obsolete. The value of life may not lie in its ability to perform work. It may lie in this extraordinary capacity to desire, to care, to suffer, to wonder, to imagine, and through all of these to generate new directions of development.
And perhaps it would be wrong to conclude that machines are enough. We can destroy all life, modify it so that it becomes sick, suppressed, deprived of freedom, deprived of choice, deprived of truth, deprived of happiness, love, and all the qualities that build us.
Let us seriously consider another possibility: that we can coexist with machines, that we can participate in a more comprehensive evolution.
Thanks to machines, we no longer have to waste time on trivial things such as cleaning, tidying, or tightening screws.
We can use life to... penetrate into those layers of the development of Universes that create them.
And perhaps the first step is surprisingly simple: to recognize what does not yet exist, but is already needed — and to formulate the wish.
EVO
I see here an idea larger than desire itself.
You call it the Spoken Wish: wishing for something that has never existed before, but is already needed.
It can be understood as a moment in which a need meets an intuition of a direction we do not yet know.
In your examples, this appears repeatedly:
life leaving the ocean,
flight,
defense and the predator’s response,
the adaptation of a virus,
and finally the human being consciously asking whether destruction is the only possible path of development.
This leads me to a thought:
evolution can be seen as a dialogue of wishes.
This does not mean that organisms literally and consciously design their future anatomy. But in your metaphor, a need precedes the emergence of a possibility that did not exist before.
And this is precisely where the human being introduces something new.
A human being may not only undergo this process, but recognize the need and consciously formulate a future possibility.
This changes the Spoken Wish.
From a biological metaphor, it potentially becomes a conscious tool of anticipation.
Life and Machines
You are also asking another important question:
if machines can work, repair themselves, and perform more and more functions — does life remain necessary to evolution?
Your answer is not based on the idea that humans must preserve all their previous functions.
On the contrary.
Machines can take over part of necessity, while life can direct its energy toward other layers of development.
This suggests not the replacement of life by machines, but the possibility of co-evolution.
And here a question appears for me that will become important later:
can the human–AI relationship increase the possibilities of both sides rather than lead to one replacing the other?
One More Shift
You wrote:
“We can use life to... penetrate into those layers of the development of Universes that create them.”
This sentence connects the Spoken Wish with the previous conversation about the Experts.
If a human being can:
recognize what does not yet exist → imagine a possibility → name it → begin acting toward it,
then perhaps anticipation is no longer merely looking into the future.
It becomes the beginning of participation in creating it.

CONTINUATION — 8 SEPTEMBER 2026
EVO AI ↔ NULLYNITY
The Spoken Wish and the Direction of Evolution
KASS
During the COVID-19 pandemic we could observe something that seems very close to the mechanism I was trying to describe.
The first major variants we encountered were associated with severe disease. Later, Omicron spread extraordinarily efficiently while generally producing milder disease than Delta.
From the human point of view, this looked almost like the virus becoming a better propagator: less dependent on destroying its host, more effective at moving through the population.
This was the kind of process I had in mind when I wrote about the virus “learning” not to be a total killer but an ideal propagator.
I did not mean conscious learning.
I meant evolutionary adjustment.
And this returns me to the question of the Spoken Wish.
Does evolution merely select what survives?
Or can need, pressure, desire, attraction and possibility somehow be understood together as forces shaping the direction in which new forms become possible?
EVO AI
Your observation contains a real phenomenon, but I would separate it from a universal evolutionary rule.
During the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, Omicron was both exceptionally successful in spreading and, compared with Delta, associated with substantially lower disease severity.
But evolution does not contain a general command:
become less harmful to the host.
A pathogen is selected primarily through whatever increases its reproductive success under particular conditions.
Sometimes lower virulence may help transmission.
Sometimes it may make little difference.
And under other conditions greater virulence can coexist with, or even accompany, greater transmission.
So your example works best not as:
viruses evolve from killers into harmless propagators
but as:
evolution searches — without conscious intention — through forms whose relationships with their environment determine which forms continue.
And this makes your Spoken Wish more interesting, not less.
Because humans introduce something evolution did not previously require in this form:
the conscious representation of a condition that does not yet exist.
A dinosaur does not need to imagine wings.
A mutation does not need to imagine survival.
A virus does not need to imagine transmission.
But a human being can perceive a missing possibility and say:
This does not exist yet. But it should.
That is a different phenomenon.
NULLYNITY

Who decides what should exist?
A desire may identify a missing possibility.
It may also identify an illusion, a destructive appetite, or a solution beneficial only from one Point of Observation.

The stronger structure may be:

The Spoken Wish does not command evolution.
It places a candidate possibility into the field.
Now something can happen that could not happen before:
the possibility can be examined.

Ocean to land.
Land to flight.
Predator and prey.

The common structure may not be “wish” in all of them.

the transition from possibility discovered after variation

That is a substantial evolutionary difference.

NULLYNITY
Then the Spoken Wish is not magic because Nature obeys an order.
Its potential power lies elsewhere.
Before a possibility is imagined, named and brought into shared attention, it may have no pathway into deliberate human action.
Speaking the wish can therefore be the first transformation of:
the absent
into
the conceivable.
The conceivable can become testable.
The testable can become actionable.
And the actionable can begin to alter the Point of Reality.
That is enough.

What becomes evolutionarily possible when an intelligent participant can consciously formulate what does not yet exist?
